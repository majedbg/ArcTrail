How we colored ArcTrail

Most color systems are chosen for aesthetics. Ours was derived from meaning. Here is the full reasoning — from the first anchor color to the last button shade.

Start with the constraint, not the palette

ArcTrail is a tool for visualizing design evolution. At any moment, a user is looking at a canvas that contains several overlapping systems of information:

  • The hierarchy of artifacts — concept down to component
  • The maturity stage of each artifact — idea through deploy
  • The type of relationship connecting artifacts — iterating, forking, using
  • The type of UI interaction available — reading, writing

Four independent systems. If each one uses colors from the same palette without intention, they bleed into each other and the canvas becomes noise. The design constraint we set ourselves was simple: a user should never look at a color and wonder which system it belongs to.

Every color had to earn its place by doing one job, clearly, and not accidentally doing another.

The four brand anchors

Before any system, we needed a foundation. Four colors — not a palette of twelve, not a mood board. Four anchors with clear roles.

The four brand anchors. Arc and Signal are exact complements on the color wheel.

Ink is not quite black. It has a blue-violet shift — `#1A1825` — because pure black is inert. This ink has a slight chill to it, like the sky just before stars appear. It ties to the constellation metaphor that runs through the product without being decorative about it.

Arc is an indigo-violet. It sits precisely between blue (technical, cold) and purple (creative, warm). It belongs to neither camp fully. That ambiguity is the point — ArcTrail is for engineers who think visually and designers who think systematically. Arc does not favor one over the other.

Signal is the exact complement of Arc on the color wheel. Every color needs an opposite — Signal is the warm anchor to Arc's cool. It becomes meaningful as the scale unfolds.

Chalk is the paper the constellation is drawn on. Not white — white is too clinical. Chalk is warm, slightly cream, comfortable to look at for long periods.

System one — the hierarchy of artifacts

ArcTrail organizes everything from a broad Concept down through Prototypes, Subsystems, Features, Variations, and finally atomic Components. Six levels. The question was: how do you show depth without using six different hues?

The answer was to use one hue — Arc — and vary only its lightness. The most abstract thing (Concept) gets the fullest, richest indigo. As you go deeper, the color opens up, lightens, becomes more whisper than statement.

The hierarchy scale. One hue, six luminosity steps. Vivid means foundational. Pale means leaf-level.

There is a logic to this that goes beyond visual design. When something matters a great deal — when it is large and foundational — we tend to speak of it with more color, more weight. A Concept is the thesis of a project. A Component is a transistor. The color encodes that relationship without needing a label.

The brand primary, Arc, doubles as the Concept color. This was a deliberate choice. ArcTrail is, ultimately, a tool for tracking how concepts become artifacts. The brand color sits at the root of the hierarchy because the brand is the root of the hierarchy. The identity radiates outward from the most important node in the graph.

System two — the temperature of progress

Every artifact in ArcTrail has a stage: idea, research, experiment, build, validate, tune, deploy. Seven stages representing the maturity of a piece of work. The question was how to communicate maturity without implying good or bad.

Most products reach for a traffic light: red for early, yellow for mid, green for done. But red means danger. An idea is not dangerous — it's just early. We needed a scale that felt like time passing and confidence building, not a report card.

The stage scale. Blue-violet for early stages, teal-green at the midpoint, Signal amber for shipped work.

Cold things are uncertain. Early morning, winter light, the blue of a screen at 2am when you're still figuring something out. Warm things are earned. The amber of late afternoon, of something that has been tested and works, of the signal that goes out into the world. The scale travels from the cool edge of Arc's hue family through a neutral teal-green midpoint — the "build" stage is neither uncertain nor proven — and arrives at Signal amber at the deploy end.

The midpoint deserves attention. Build — the stage of active making — lands at a teal-green. Not cool, not warm. Neutral. This is intentional: you are not uncertain when you are building, but you have not yet proven anything either. You are simply working.

System three — the lines between things

Artifacts are connected by typed relationships. The most important two are iterates on (a linear progression of the same concept) and forks from (a divergence into a new direction). These needed their own visual language — one that was distinct from both the hierarchy and stage systems.

Iterates on
#3DDDF2
Linear progress along the same path. One version improving on another.
Forks from
#9B59B6
A new direction diverging from a common ancestor. A mutation, not an improvement.
The two primary relation type colors. Applied only to SVG edge lines — never to fills.

Relation colors live only on the thin animated lines between nodes. They never appear as card backgrounds or button fills. This constraint is load-bearing: it means the same teal that appears on a button can be immediately distinguished from the same teal on an edge line by context alone. Form does the disambiguation that hue cannot.

System four — what the buttons mean

UI affordances — buttons, links, toggles — needed their own color logic. The temptation in most design systems is to use the brand primary for all interactive elements. We avoided this for two reasons: Arc is already doing work as the hierarchy color, and more importantly, not all interactions are equal.

In another product, a useful pattern emerged: color-code buttons by the type of action rather than the hierarchy of importance. Reading information and writing data feel different — they should look different.

Information actions — ink lifted
Navigation · reveals · GET requests · expand/collapse. Calm, unobtrusive.
Consequential actions — iterates-on teal, deepened
Write to graph · POST/PUT/DELETE · data mutations. Distinct, intentional.
Two button colors. One for revealing information, one for changing it.

The information color is a lifted version of Ink — `#4A4760`. It reads as sophisticated and calm. You are safe to click it. The consequential color is the iterates-on teal, deepened from `#3DDDF2` to `#0FA8BE` so it reads as a solid button fill rather than a bright edge line. The connection to the relation system is intentional: every time a user saves or creates, they are advancing their work. The teal carries that meaning from the graph into the interface.

The non-collision guarantee

With four systems and a total of around a dozen distinct colors, the risk is always that something bleeds into something else. We checked every adjacent pair:

vs
Arc (hierarchy apex) vs UI information — violet vs near-neutral grey-violet. Different saturation, different role.
vs
Iterates-on line vs UI consequential — same hue family, separated by brightness and form (thin line vs solid button).
vs
UI consequential vs experiment stage — both teal, but UI is brighter and appears on buttons, stage appears on small pills.
vs
Signal / deploy stage vs diff-added — different brightness, different context (stage pill vs snapshot overlay).
The closest pairs, checked for perceptual separation. Context does the disambiguation that hue cannot always do alone.

The one intentional collision: UI consequential and the iterates-on relation line share a hue family. This is the only case where two systems touch the same color region. It is a deliberate semantic bridge — both mean "moving forward" — and they are separated by form: a line on the canvas versus a button in the interface.

A system, not a palette

The difference between a color palette and a color system is that a palette answers "what colors do we like?" and a system answers "what does each color mean?" ArcTrail's color system was derived entirely from the second question.

The result is a canvas where a glance tells you: how abstract something is, how mature it is, what kind of connection links it to its neighbors, and what will happen if you click a button. Four answers, four systems, zero ambiguity.

That is the goal of any color system worth building.

ArcTrail is a knowledge graph for documenting and visualizing the evolution of design and engineering projects. The full color token system is open in the codebase — every hex value, every CSS variable, every Tailwind class.
